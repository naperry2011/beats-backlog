I wrote about Baldur's Gate 3 recently and landed on the idea that its real
cost isn't hours, it's continuity. It wants your evenings consecutive, and
punishes you for a two-week gap by making you do archaeology on your own save
file.

This is the other side of that, and it's the reason a genre a lot of people
consider lesser keeps quietly winning my actual year.

Time Stranger does not care how long you were gone.

<Divider />

The reasons are unglamorous and entirely structural.

There's a quest log, and it tells you the thing. Not a vibe, not a journal
entry you have to interpret, just the next place to go. Turn-based combat
means nothing lives in your hands, so two weeks off costs you no skill you had
built. And the systems, the raising and the evolution requirements and the
stat-shaping, are all sitting in menus that will still be there, laid out the
same way, whenever you get back. Nothing about this game is stored in your
head. It's all stored in the game.

That sounds like faint praise. It isn't. It's the single most valuable
property a long game can have for someone whose evenings are decided by other
people, and almost nothing with real production budget behind it bothers to
have it.

The other half is that it's a Digimon game, and I was the right age at the
right time, so there's a direct line running back to being a kid about this
that I'm not going to pretend is objective. A game about going back and
sorting out the past is a fairly on-the-nose thing for a man in his thirties
to be enjoying this much. I'm aware. It didn't stop me.

## What it actually costs you

The bill comes in menu time, and I want to be honest about that because the
column is worthless if I only tell you the good part.

A big chunk of the fifty hours is spent in interfaces. Managing, evolving,
checking requirements, reshuffling a team, walking numbers up. If that reads
as tedium to you, it will be tedium, and no amount of nostalgia will convert
it. The story is uneven in the way these usually are, moving between genuinely
good stretches and long patches of people explaining things to each other.

And it is not prestige. It won't be in anybody's conversation at the end of
the year, the writing is not going to knock you over, and the presentation is
solid rather than remarkable.

So it's not the best game I played. It's the one I actually finished, in the
life I actually have, in twenty-minute pieces spread across weeks, without
ever once opening it and thinking "wait, where was I."

<Divider />

That gap between the best game and the right game is the entire reason this
column exists.

Baldur's Gate 3 is a better piece of work than this by a distance I'm not
going to insult you by pretending isn't there. But it wanted a version of my
schedule I don't have, and this one met me where I was, and only one of them
is finished.

Worth your hours is not a question about quality. It's a question about fit.
