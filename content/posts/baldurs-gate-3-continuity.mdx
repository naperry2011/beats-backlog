On paper this is the most tired-parent-friendly big game ever made, and I want
to give it that credit before I take anything back.

It's turn based, which means it waits. Combat pauses in the middle and stays
paused. Nobody punishes you for setting the controller down because somebody
needs water. There is no reflex requirement, no twitch skill you lost a
console generation ago, no sequence you have to run cleanly on the fourth try.
You can play it at sixty percent attention and still play it well, and there
are not many games with this much ambition you can say that about.

You can also save anywhere. That sounds small. It isn't. Half of what makes a
game hostile to an unpredictable evening is the distance between where you are
and the next place the game lets you stop.

So by the usual measures of this column, it should sail through.

<Divider />

Here's where it gets complicated.

The cost of this game is not hours. It's continuity. And continuity is the one
currency a tired adult has even less of than time.

The reason to play Baldur's Gate 3 at all is its reactivity. It remembers. It
remembers who you spoke to, who you let die, what you promised somebody in a
tent two acts ago, which door you avoided. That web is the entire point, and
it's genuinely remarkable when you're inside it.

But a web that remembers everything you did only pays off if *you* remember
what you did.

Put it down for two weeks, which is what actually happens to a game like this
in a house with a bedtime, and you come back to a save file with eleven active
quests, six companions with opinions you can't reconstruct, a bag of items you
apparently thought were important, and a strong feeling you were on your way
somewhere. The game has not gotten harder. It has gotten opaque. You spend
your first forty minutes back not playing but doing archaeology on your own
decisions.

And then next week you do it again, because the session where you rebuilt your
context was the session, and now it's been another eleven days.

That is the failure mode. Not difficulty, not length. It's that fragmented
play quietly strips out the exact thing you came for, until you're pushing a
save file around out of obligation and calling it a backlog.

## So what do you actually do

Three honest options.

Play it in blocks. Not one night a week for eight months. Three nights close
together, then a break, then three more. Momentum is worth more here than
total hours, and this is the rare game where playing less often makes it worse
rather than just slower.

Or take notes. I know how that sounds. But two lines in your phone at the end
of a session about where you were headed and who you just annoyed will save
you the entire archaeology tax next time. It feels ridiculous for about a
week, and then it feels obvious.

Or, and this is a real answer and not a cop-out: wait. Some games are better
in a season of life that has not arrived yet. This one is not going anywhere,
it will not be patched into a worse version, and a year from now you might
have a run of evenings that actually fit it. Playing it badly now costs you
the version of it that's worth playing.

<Divider />

None of this is a knock on the game. It's the best thing of its kind, and if
you have the nights, it's the easy call.

But this column is not about whether something is good. It's about whether it
earns the hours you actually have, and Baldur's Gate 3 is specific about the
shape of those hours in a way most games aren't. It doesn't want a lot of your
time. It wants your time in a row.

Give it that and it's the best money you'll spend. Give it scraps and it turns
into homework.
