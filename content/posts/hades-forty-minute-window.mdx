The question this column asks is not whether something is good. Plenty of
things are good. The question is whether it earns the ninety minutes between
the last light going off down the hall and the point where tomorrow starts to
hurt.

Hades earns it, and it earns it structurally, which is rarer than it sounds.

Most games that respect your time do it by being small. Short runs, small
scope, nothing much lost if you put it down. That works, but it usually means
settling for less game. Hades pulls a different trick: it stays big, and makes
every stopping point a real one. A run is twenty to forty minutes. You die, and
dying is not a rewind. The upgrades you bought stay bought. The story moves.
Somebody in the house has a new thing to say to you.

That last part is the whole design, and it's the part other games in the genre
keep missing. Failure is the delivery mechanism. You are not grinding toward
the story, you are being handed it in exchange for losing, which means the
worst version of your evening still pays out.

<Divider />

Here's what that means at eleven at night, practically.

You do not have to remember anything. There's no thread to pick back up, no
quest log to re-read, no "wait, where was I going." You start at the same
place, walk through the same door, and the game hands you a fresh set of
decisions. For a brain that spent the day holding forty other things, being
asked to hold nothing is the feature.

You also do not have to be good. I want to be honest about that, because a lot
of writing about this game comes from people with faster hands than mine. The
difficulty is real, but the progression is generous enough that persistence
substitutes for skill. Show up enough nights, you get through. That is a
reasonable trade for someone whose reflexes peaked during a different console
generation.

## The part that costs you

Now the counterweight, because a column called Worth Your Hours? that never
mentions the bill is just an ad.

Hades is dangerous precisely because it's so well built for short sessions. A
run ends cleanly. That's the pitch. But a run ending cleanly at 10:40 also
means there is a clean, obvious, completely reasonable slot for another one,
and the next one is only twenty minutes, and you can feel the build coming
together this time.

The game is not disrespecting your time. It's doing something slipperier: it
removes every natural excuse to stop. Games that sprawl let you quit out of
fatigue. This one never gets tiring enough to save you from yourself. The
structure protects your evening; nothing protects you from the structure.

So the honest recommendation comes with a piece of tape on it. Decide the
number of runs before you start. Two is a night. Three is a late night. Four
and you have made a choice about tomorrow, and you should at least make it on
purpose.

<Divider />

There is a version of this piece that just says "great game, play it," and it
would not be wrong. But that's not useful to anyone standing in the kitchen at
ten o'clock deciding whether to open the laptop.

So: it is worth your hours, more than almost anything else with this shape. It
gives you something for every session, including the bad ones. It asks nothing
of your memory. It has the best excuse for a bad run of any game I can think
of, which is that a bad run is still the story moving forward.

Just know going in that the thing making it perfect for a tired adult is the
same thing that will keep you up. Set the alarm. Put it across the room.
